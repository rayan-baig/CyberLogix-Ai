An alert that can be slept through is not an alert.

Most monitoring stops at the notification. It sends a message into a channel and considers the job done. This one does not stop until a person has said, out loud, that they have it.

The moment it happens

Scored, not just recorded

Every reading is judged against the limits for that sector — a blood bank and a wine cellar do not fail at the same temperature — and a breach opens exactly one incident, however many times the sensor reports it.

Within seconds

The whole roster is texted

Not one number on file. Everyone on call for that site, at once, with the asset, the reading and the site named in the first line — so it can be acted on from a lock screen.

If nobody answers

The phone rings

A spoken call that reads out what is failing and asks the person to press 1. Until somebody does, it is not acknowledged, and the system keeps behaving as though nobody knows.

The alert is the easy half.

What a business is really exposed to is the week afterwards: the insurer, the auditor, the regulator, the customer who wants to know what happened. That is where a monitoring bill justifies itself.

A record that cannot be edited

Every entry is chained to the one before it, so a reading cannot be removed or altered after the fact without the chain saying so. The value of a log in a dispute is exactly the difficulty of faking it.

The claim packet, already written

One button produces the document a loss adjuster asks for: the readings either side of the failure, when the alert went out, who acknowledged it and how long it took them.

Proof it was working

A signed attestation that a given sensor was reporting, and was being watched by a named person, over a given window — for the audit that happens when nothing went wrong at all.
